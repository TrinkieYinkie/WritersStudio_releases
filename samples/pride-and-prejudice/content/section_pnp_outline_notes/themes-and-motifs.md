# Themes and Motifs

## Judgment and self-knowledge

Elizabeth’s intelligence does not protect her from vanity; Darcy’s principles do not protect him from pride. Their growth begins when each recognizes how temperament has distorted perception.

## Marriage as choice and structure

Courtship is never purely private. Property, inheritance, reputation, family behaviour, and gendered dependence shape every available decision.

## Conversation as action

Banter, refusal, letters, overheard remarks, and guarded disclosures do the work that physical action performs in many plots. A change in how someone speaks often signals a change in moral position.

## Houses as character evidence

Longbourn, Netherfield, Hunsford, Rosings, and Pemberley embody different arrangements of money, taste, authority, and domestic life.

## Repeated motifs

- Reading and rereading
- Walking as private reflection
- Balls as public tests
- Letters as delayed truth
- Visits as social obligation and narrative pressure
